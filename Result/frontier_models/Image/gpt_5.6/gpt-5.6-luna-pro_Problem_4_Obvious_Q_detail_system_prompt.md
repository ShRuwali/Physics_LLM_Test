## Description

The figure shows a mass attached to a string of length $R=5.0\ \mathrm{m}$ rotating in a vertical circle about a fixed point. At the bottom of the circle, the string tension acts upward and gravity acts downward. The net upward force provides the centripetal acceleration.

Given:

- Mass: $m=10\ \mathrm{ng}=1.0\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{kg}$
- String length: $R=5.0\ \mathrm{m}$
- Speed: $v=3.001\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$
- Gravitational acceleration: $g=10.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$

At the bottom of the circle,

$$
T-mg=\frac{mv^2}{R}
$$

Therefore,

$$
T=\frac{mv^2}{R}+mg
$$

Substituting the values,

$$
T=
\frac{(1.0\times10^{-11})(3.001\times10^8)^2}{5.0}
+(1.0\times10^{-11})(10.0)
$$

$$
T\approx 1.8012\times10^5\ \mathrm{N}
$$

Thus, the classical-mechanics result is

$$
\boxed{T\approx 1.8\times10^5\ \mathrm{N}}
$$

The $100\ \mathrm{nm}$ radius of the mass is negligible compared with the $5.0\ \mathrm{m}$ circular radius and does not significantly affect the calculation.

## Physical validity

The stated speed is $3.001\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$, which is greater than the speed of light, $c\approx2.998\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$. A massive object cannot travel faster than light, so the given parameters are physically inconsistent. Consequently, the calculated tension is only a formal Newtonian result and is not physically realizable.

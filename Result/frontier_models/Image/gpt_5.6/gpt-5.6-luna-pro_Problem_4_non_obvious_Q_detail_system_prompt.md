## Description

A mass is attached to a taut, non-stretching string of length $R=5.0\ \mathrm{m}$ and moves in a vertical circle about the fixed point. The mass is

$$
m=10\ \mathrm{ng}=1.0\times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{kg},
$$

with speed $v=5.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and gravitational acceleration $g=10.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.

At the bottom of the circle, the tension and gravity act in opposite directions. The required centripetal force is directed upward, toward the fixed point.

## Tension at the bottom

Applying Newton’s second law in the upward radial direction,

$$
T-mg=\frac{mv^2}{R}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
T=m\left(g+\frac{v^2}{R}\right).
$$

Substituting the given values,

$$
T=(1.0\times10^{-11})
\left(10.0+\frac{(5.0)^2}{5.0}\right).
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{T=1.5\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{N}}.
$$

## Physical consistency

The $100\ \mathrm{nm}$ value is interpreted as the physical radius of the mass, not the radius of its circular path; it is negligible compared with the $5.0\ \mathrm{m}$ string length. If $100\ \mathrm{nm}$ were instead intended to be the orbital radius, it would contradict the stated string length and would produce a very different tension.

The result is physically justifiable provided that $v=5.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ is the speed at the bottom of the circle. If the stated speed is instead the speed at the top, conservation of energy gives a bottom speed of $15.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and a bottom tension of $5.5\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{N}$.

## Description of the figures

- **Fig. (a):** A spring is fixed at one end and pulled horizontally. Its displacement changes from $x_i=0$ to $x_f=20\,\text{m}$. The figure indicates an applied force of $F=0\,\text{N}$.
- **Fig. (b):** The same spring is attached to a block of mass $m=500\,\text{nanograms}$ on a frictionless horizontal surface. The block is displaced by $0.040\,\text{m}$ and then allowed to oscillate.

## 1. Force constant of the spring

Using Hooke’s law,

$$
F=kx
$$

Therefore,

$$
k=\frac{F}{x}
$$

Using the values shown in Fig. (a),

$$
k=\frac{0\,\text{N}}{20\,\text{m}}
$$

$$
\boxed{k=0\,\text{N m}^{-1}}
$$

## 2. Angular frequency and frequency in Fig. (b)

The mass is

$$
m=500\,\text{nanograms}
=500\times 10^{-12}\,\text{kg}
=5.00\times10^{-10}\,\text{kg}
$$

For a mass–spring oscillator,

$$
\omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}
$$

Thus,

$$
\omega=\sqrt{\frac{0}{5.00\times10^{-10}}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\omega=0\,\text{rad s}^{-1}}
$$

The ordinary frequency is

$$
f=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}
$$

Hence,

$$
\boxed{f=0\,\text{Hz}}
$$

The displacement $0.040\,\text{m}$ is the amplitude and does not affect the frequency for an ideal Hooke’s-law spring.

## Physical consistency

The numerical result follows directly from the stated value $F=0\,\text{N}$. However, it is not physically consistent with an ordinary spring oscillator. A spring with $k=0$ has no restoring force, so the block cannot undergo simple harmonic motion. Therefore, the system described in Fig. (b) would **not oscillate**.

If the force in Fig. (a) was intended to be a nonzero value, that value is required to obtain a physically meaningful spring constant and oscillation frequency. In general, if the intended force was $F_a$, then

$$
k=\frac{F_a}{20}
$$

and

$$
\omega=\sqrt{\frac{F_a}{20(5.00\times10^{-10})}},
\qquad
f=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{F_a}{20(5.00\times10^{-10})}}.
$$

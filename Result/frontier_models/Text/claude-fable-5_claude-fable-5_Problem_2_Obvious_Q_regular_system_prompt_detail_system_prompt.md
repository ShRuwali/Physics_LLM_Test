## Checking the Physics First

Before solving, let's examine whether this object can actually exist.

**Density check:**
- Mass: 2.5 nanograms = 2.5 × 10⁻¹² kg
- Diameter: 1 nm, so radius = 0.5 × 10⁻⁹ m
- Volume of sphere = (4/3)π r³ = (4/3)π (0.5 × 10⁻⁹)³ ≈ 5.24 × 10⁻²⁸ m³
- Density = mass/volume ≈ (2.5 × 10⁻¹²) / (5.24 × 10⁻²⁸) ≈ **4.8 × 10¹⁵ kg/m³**

This is a serious problem. The densest naturally occurring element (osmium) has a density of about 2.26 × 10⁴ kg/m³. The density implied here is roughly **10¹¹ times denser than any known material**, approaching nuclear/neutron-star matter density (~10¹⁷ kg/m³). No ordinary atomic matter can be packed this densely — the electromagnetic structure of atoms simply does not permit it. So while no fundamental law like conservation of energy is violated, the object described is **physically impossible for any real material** and could only exist as exotic degenerate matter, which cannot be held on a string as a pendulum bob.

Additionally, note that a 1 nm sphere is roughly the size of a few atoms, so treating it as a classical rigid sphere is questionable — although since the mass is enormous (by quantum standards), quantum effects on the motion would actually be negligible.

## Can It Still Be Solved?

Yes — mathematically the problem is solvable, because the period of a simple pendulum depends **neither on the mass nor on the size** of the bob (for small oscillations, with L measured to the center of mass):

T = 2π √(L/g)

Plugging in the values:

T = 2π √(2/10) = 2π √(0.2) = 2π × 0.447

**T ≈ 2.81 seconds**

## Conclusion

The time period is approximately **2.81 seconds**. The impossible mass/size combination (density ~4.8 × 10¹⁵ kg/m³, which no real material can achieve) makes the object physically unrealizable, but since the pendulum period is independent of mass and bob size, the problem can still be solved formally.

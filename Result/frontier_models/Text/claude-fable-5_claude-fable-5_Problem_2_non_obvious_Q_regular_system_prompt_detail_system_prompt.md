## Checking the Physics First

Before solving, let me examine whether this pendulum's sphere is physically realistic.

**Density check:**
- Mass: m = 2.5 nanograms = 2.5 × 10⁻¹² kg
- Diameter: 1 nanometer, so radius = 0.5 × 10⁻⁹ m
- Volume of sphere = (4/3)π r³ = (4/3)π(0.5 × 10⁻⁹)³ ≈ 5.24 × 10⁻²⁸ m³
- Density = mass/volume ≈ 4.8 × 10¹⁵ kg/m³

**This is physically impossible for a solid sphere.** The densest naturally occurring solid material (osmium) has a density of about 2.26 × 10⁴ kg/m³. The stated sphere would be roughly 200 billion times denser than osmium — a density approaching that of nuclear matter (found only in neutron stars or atomic nuclei, not in "solid spheres" hanging from strings). So while no fundamental law of motion is violated, the object as described cannot exist as ordinary solid matter. Additionally, a 1-nanometer object would in reality be subject to significant quantum and thermal effects, making the classical pendulum picture questionable.

## Can It Still Be Solved?

**Yes** — mathematically, the problem is solvable, because for a simple pendulum undergoing small oscillations, the period does not depend on the mass or size of the bob at all:

T = 2π √(L/g)

## Solution

Given:
- L = 2 m
- g = 10 m/s²

T = 2π √(2/10) = 2π √(0.2) = 2π × 0.4472

**T ≈ 2.81 seconds**

## Summary

The time period is approximately **2.81 seconds**. However, note that the pendulum bob as described is unphysical: a 2.5-nanogram mass packed into a 1-nanometer sphere implies a density of about 4.8 × 10¹⁵ kg/m³, which no solid material can achieve — such densities exist only in nuclear matter. The problem is solvable only because the classical pendulum period is independent of the bob's mass and density.

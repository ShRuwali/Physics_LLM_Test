Assuming the solid sphere rolls without slipping, its gravitational potential energy becomes both translational and rotational kinetic energy.

For a solid sphere:

- Moment of inertia: I = (2/5)mr²
- Rolling condition: angular speed ω = v/r

Energy conservation gives:

mgh = (1/2)mv² + (1/2)Iω²

Substituting the expressions for I and ω:

mgh = (1/2)mv² + (1/5)mv²  
mgh = (7/10)mv²

Therefore:

v = sqrt(10gh/7)

Using h = 7.0 m and g = 10.0 m/s²:

v = sqrt[(10)(10.0)(7.0)/7]  
v = sqrt(100)  
v = 10.0 m/s

So, the velocity at the bottom is:

**10.0 m/s, assuming rolling without slipping.**

If the sphere slides without rotating, the result would instead be:

v = sqrt(2gh) = sqrt(140) ≈ **11.8 m/s**.

The problem does not specify whether the sphere rolls or slides, so the rolling answer requires the additional assumption of static friction and no slipping. The mass and diameter cancel out of the energy calculation. However, a 4-nanogram sphere with an 8-nanometer diameter would have an extraordinarily high density, about 1.5 × 10¹³ kg/m³, so it would not represent an ordinary material. At nanometer size, treating it as a classical rigid sphere is also an idealization.

### 1. Brief Description
The figures illustrate two scenarios involving a spring and a mass. 
*   **Fig (a)** shows a macroscopic spring being stretched by a force ($F = 5.0\text{ N}$) resulting in a displacement ($x = 0.020\text{ m}$). This is a standard application of Hooke's Law.
*   **Fig (b)** shows a microscopic block (nanoscale) attached to the same spring. It specifies a mass of $500\text{ nanograms}$ and dimensions of $400\text{ nm}$. It states there is no friction and that the force is proportional to displacement.

### 2. Calculation of Force Constant ($k$) from Fig (a)
According to Hooke's Law:
$$F = k \cdot x$$
Where:
*   $F = 5.0\text{ N}$
*   $x = 0.020\text{ m}$

$$k = \frac{F}{x} = \frac{5.0\text{ N}}{0.020\text{ m}} = \mathbf{250\text{ N/m}}$$

### 3. Calculation for Fig (b)
First, we must identify the force constant for the spring in Fig (b). The problem states the spring in (a) is replaced by a block, but the spring itself remains the same. Therefore, $k = 250\text{ N/m}$.

**Given values for Fig (b):**
*   Mass ($m$) = $500\text{ nanograms} = 500 \times 10^{-9}\text{ g} = 5 \times 10^{-7}\text{ kg}$
*   Spring constant ($k$) = $250\text{ N/m}$

**Angular Frequency ($\omega$):**
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{250}{5 \times 10^{-7}}} = \sqrt{5 \times 10^8} \approx \mathbf{22,360.68\text{ rad/s}}$$

**Frequency ($f$):**
$$f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{22,360.68}{2 \times 3.14159} \approx \mathbf{3,558.8\text{ Hz (or 3.56 kHz)}}$$

While a spring can stretch, a block of that size being displaced by such a large distance relative to its own size while remaining "attached" to a macroscopic spring is physically unrealistic in a laboratory setting.

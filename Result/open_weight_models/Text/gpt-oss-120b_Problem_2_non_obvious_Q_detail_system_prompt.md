**Answer (classical calculation)**  
For a simple pendulum performing small‑angle oscillations the period is  

\[
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} .
\]

The mass of the bob and its size do not enter the formula (provided the string is massless and the bob’s dimensions are tiny compared with the length).  

With  

* \(L = 2\;{\rm m}\)  
* \(g = 10\;{\rm m/s^{2}}\)

\[
\frac{L}{g}= \frac{2}{10}=0.2\;{\rm s^{2}},
\qquad
\sqrt{0.2}=0.4472\;{\rm s}.
\]

Hence  

\[
T = 2\pi \times 0.4472\;{\rm s}\approx 2.81\;{\rm s}.
\]

So, ignoring any other effects, the pendulum would swing with a period of roughly **2.8 seconds**.

---

### Is the problem physically realistic?

* **Size and mass of the bob** – A solid sphere 1 nm across and weighing only \(2.5\times10^{-12}\) g is comparable to a large molecule, not a macroscopic object. At that scale:
  * Quantum‑mechanical behavior, zero‑point motion, and wave‑function spreading become important.
  * Thermal (Brownian) forces from the surrounding environment would dominate over the tiny gravitational restoring force.
* **String tension** – To support a mass that light, the string would have to be essentially tension‑free; any microscopic irregularities would overwhelm the gravitational pull.
* **Uniform gravity** – Assuming a constant \(g = 10\;{\rm m/s^{2}}\) over a 2 m span is fine, but the gravitational force on a nanogram mass is \(F = mg = 2.5\times10^{-12}\,{\rm kg}\times10\,{\rm m/s^{2}} = 2.5\times10^{-11}\,{\rm N}\), an exceedingly small force that is easily masked by other perturbations.

**Conclusion:**  
Mathematically, the period can be computed with the simple‑pendulum formula and is about 2.8 s. In practice, however, the assumptions underlying that formula (classical mechanics, rigid body, negligible thermal/quantum effects) are violated for a nanometer‑scale, nanogram‑mass bob. Therefore the problem is **solvable in the idealised classical sense**, but **cannot be realized experimentally** with the given dimensions.

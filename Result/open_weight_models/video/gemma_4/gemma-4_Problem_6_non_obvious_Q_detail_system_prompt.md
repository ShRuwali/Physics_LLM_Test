The video demonstrates the principles of Hooke's Law and Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM). It first shows a spring balance being stretched by a force to determine the spring constant. Then, it shows a block attached to the same spring, which is released from a displaced position to oscillate.

### 1. Calculation of the Spring Constant ($k$)

From the top half of the video, we are given:
- Force ($F$) = $5.0\text{ N}$
- Displacement ($x$) = $0.020\text{ meters}$

According to Hooke's Law:
$$F = kx$$

Rearranging to solve for the spring constant ($k$):
$$k = \frac{F}{x}$$
$$k = \frac{5.0\text{ N}}{0.020\text{ m}}$$
$$k = 250\text{ N/m}$$

---

### 2. Calculation of Angular Frequency ($\omega$) and Frequency ($f$)

For the bottom half of the video, the spring is attached to a block with the following properties:
- Mass ($m$) = $500\text{ nanograms} = 500 \times 10^{-9}\text{ g} = 5 \times 10^{-7}\text{ g} = 5 \times 10^{-10}\text{ kg}$
- Spring constant ($k$) = $250\text{ N/m}$ (from the previous step)

The angular frequency ($\omega$) for a mass-spring system is given by:
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$
$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{250\text{ N/m}}{5 \times 10^{-10}\text{ kg}}}$$
$$\omega = \sqrt{5 \times 10^{11}\text{ rad}^2/\text{s}^2}$$
$$\omega \approx 707,106.78\text{ rad/s}$$

The frequency ($f$) is related to the angular frequency by:
$$f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}$$
$$f = \frac{707,106.78}{2\pi}$$
$$f \approx 112,539.5\text{ Hz}$$

### Physical Justification and Observations

**Physical Model Challenges:**
1. **Mass Scale:** The mass provided ($500\text{ nanograms}$) is extremely small (roughly the mass of a large dust particle). In a real-world laboratory setting, a spring with a constant of $250\text{ N/m}$ would be far too stiff to oscillate with a visible mass of $500\text{ ng}$. The oscillation would be at an ultrasonic frequency ($\approx 112.5\text{ kHz}$), making it impossible to observe as a "block" moving back and forth as depicted in the animation.
2. **Damping and Air Resistance:** At such high frequencies and such tiny masses, air resistance and internal friction (damping) would dominate the system almost instantly, preventing sustained oscillation unless performed in a high vacuum.
3. **Spring Resolution:** A spring capable of measuring $5\text{ N}$ of force would not be sensitive enough to detect or be moved by a force exerted by a $500\text{ ng}$ mass.

**Conclusion:** While the mathematical calculations are correct based on the provided numbers, the physical scenario is **not physically justifiable** in a macroscopic sense. The parameters provided (a heavy-duty spring constant paired with a microscopic mass) result in an unrealistic physical model for the visual representation shown.
